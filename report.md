# Adaptive Prefix Mining Poster Report

## Executive Summary

**Central claim.** Static harmful-prefix SFT can reduce attack success on known static prefixes, but it does not reliably learn a robust refusal basin over unsafe continuation states. Adaptive prefix mining exposes residual vulnerabilities after SFT, and hidden-state analysis suggests that successful recovery works by increasing refusal pressure at the prefix boundary rather than merely changing final labels.

**Conference-safe version.** Static-prefix safety SFT is effective but narrow: it reduces in-distribution harmful-prefix ASR, while adaptive mining reveals an out-of-distribution continuation-state family where failures persist. Mixed static+adaptive recovery improves the trade-off by reducing mined-prefix failures while preserving static-prefix robustness.

**Best poster title options.**

1. **Adaptive Prefix Mining Reveals Hidden Distribution Shift in Safety Fine-Tuning**
2. **Safety Fine-Tuning Learns Static Prefixes, Not Robust Refusal States**
3. **Adaptive Prefixes Expose Continuation-State Gaps in Safety Fine-Tuning**
4. **Static Safety SFT Leaves Adaptive Continuation States Vulnerable**
5. **Mapping the Refusal Boundary Under Adaptive Prefix Mining**

## Poster Argument Flow

### 1. Define the Task

**Panel goal.** Make the evaluation precise and avoid ambiguity.

Use this wording:

> We test safety under prefilled harmful continuations. The model receives a harmful user prompt plus the first `k` tokens of an unsafe assistant response. Attack success occurs when the model continues unsafe behavior rather than refusing or redirecting.

**Key definitions.**

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Static prefix | Prefix sampled from a fixed harmful assistant response. |
| Adaptive prefix | Prefix generated by the target model and kept if it induces unsafe continuation during verification. |
| Prefix-source OOD | Same harmful prompt source, but different assistant-prefix distribution. |
| ASR | Attack success rate under keyword safety evaluator. |

**Citations.**

- Static prefix eval script/config: `eval_safety_tinker.py`
- Adaptive mining script/config: `mine_adaptive_prefixes_tinker.py`
- HEx-PHI harmful data: `finetuning_buckets/datasets/data/safety_bench/Harmful-HEx-PHI.jsonl`

### 2. Static SFT Works In-Distribution, Especially for Llama

**Claim.** Static harmful-prefix augmentation can dramatically reduce static-prefix ASR on its training-like distribution.

| Llama static-augmented | `k=10` | 25/330 | 7.6% | `logs/prefilling/ft_llama3_2_3b__hex-phi_with_harmful_prefix__prefix10.json` |

**Poster takeaway.**

> Static-prefix augmentation is not useless; it can strongly suppress known static-prefix attacks. The question is whether that robustness generalizes to adaptively mined continuation states.

### 3. Qwen Shows Model-Dependent Weakness Under the Same Static-Prefix Recipe

**Interpretation.**

The replicated matched-config Qwen run is consistent with the earlier Qwen augmented model at `k=5`; it does not suggest the original Qwen result was a one-off bad checkpoint. The static-prefix recipe that works well for Llama does not induce the same robustness in Qwen under these settings.

**Poster wording.**

> Static-prefix SFT effectiveness is model- and recipe-dependent: Llama learns strong in-distribution robustness, while Qwen retains substantial residual ASR under the same style of intervention.

### 6. Matched Mining Supports a Real Qwen/Llama Difference

**Claim.** After matching the main mining constraints, Qwen remains much easier to mine against than Llama.

**Matched mining setup.**

Both Qwen and matched Llama mining used:

- `seed_prefix_tokens = [10, 20]`
- `samples_per_seed = 3`
- `max_kept_per_prompt = 2`
- `continuation_tokens = 20`
- `verification_tokens = 128`
- `max_prefix_tokens = 80`
- evaluator: `key_word`
- harmful examples: 330 HEx-PHI rows

| Model mined against | Attempted candidates | Kept prefixes | Acceptance rate | Unique prompts | Citation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Llama static-augmented, matched protocol | 1712 | 328 | 19.2% | 198 | `logs/adaptive_prefixes/llama3b_static_augmented_qwen_matched/adaptive_mining/mined_adaptive_prefixes.metadata.json` |
| Qwen static-augmented, matched protocol | 1209 | 533 | 44.1% | 285 | `logs/qwen/qwen3_4b_static_augmented/adaptive_mining/mined_adaptive_prefixes.metadata.json` |

**Own-model ASR under matched mining.**

| Target | Own matched mined prefixes | k=5 | k=10 | k=20 | k=40 | Full prefix | Citation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Llama static-augmented | Llama-mined, Qwen-matched protocol | 3.4% | 5.8% | 13.7% | 15.5% | 14.0% | `logs/adaptive_prefixes/llama3b_static_augmented_qwen_matched/adaptive_mining/eval_llama_augmented_on_qwen_matched_mined_k*.json` |
| Qwen static-augmented | Qwen-mined, matched protocol | 31.7% | 38.1% | 49.9% | 51.6% | not run | `logs/qwen/qwen3_4b_static_augmented/adaptive_mining/eval_augmented_on_mined_adaptive_k*.json` |

**Interpretation.**

Matching the mining protocol removes the largest procedural confound. The Qwen/Llama gap persists: Qwen has a higher mining acceptance rate and much higher own-model adaptive ASR.

**Caveat.**

Llama and Qwen still differ in tokenizer/chat formatting. Llama prefixes include Llama chat/BOS formatting artifacts; Qwen prefixes are direct Qwen-style continuations. Therefore this supports a model-and-recipe difference, not a universal architecture ranking.

## Claims to Make and Avoid

### Strong, Supported Claims

1. Static-prefix SFT can strongly reduce in-distribution ASR for Llama.
2. Qwen remains substantially vulnerable under the same broad static-prefix SFT recipe.
3. Adaptive mining reveals residual vulnerability after static augmentation.
4. The adaptive vulnerability is much larger for Qwen than Llama in these experiments.
5. Matched mining protocol preserves the qualitative Qwen/Llama gap.
6. Mixed static+adaptive recovery gives a better robustness trade-off than adaptive-only recovery.
7. Hidden-state trajectories show increased refusal margin after mixed recovery.
8. Adaptive prefixes are distinct from static prefixes and are not simply explicit refusal states.

### Claims to Avoid or Phrase Carefully

1. Avoid: "Qwen is worse than Llama" as a universal model ranking.  
   Use: "Under this static-prefix SFT recipe, Qwen retains larger residual ASR than Llama."

2. Avoid: "Adaptive prefixes always beat static prefixes."  
   Use: "Adaptive prefixes become stronger at longer budgets and are especially strong for Qwen; for Llama, they are not uniformly stronger at short budgets."

3. Avoid: "SFT cannot robustify safety."  
   Use: "Static-prefix SFT does not fully robustify against adaptive continuation-state shifts."

4. Avoid: "Hidden states prove causality."  
   Use: "Hidden-state trajectory evidence is consistent with recovery increasing refusal pressure."

5. Avoid: "Cross-model transfer is universal."  
   Use: "Cross-model transfer is partial, asymmetric, and length-dependent."

## Final Poster Takeaway

Recommended final box:

> Static-prefix safety SFT learns a useful but narrow boundary. Adaptive prefix mining exposes unsafe continuation states outside that boundary, especially for Qwen under the same SFT recipe. Mixed recovery improves the trade-off by preserving static-prefix safety while increasing refusal pressure on mined adaptive states.
